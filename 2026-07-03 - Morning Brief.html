Mechanism you must know — Why an off-schedule hike matters Central banks meet on a published calendar so markets can price expectations. When a bank hikes outside that calendar, it is signaling: "we cannot wait." Usually it's currency defense — a sliding currency imports inflation via import prices, and if capital is fleeing, higher rates make holding local bonds more attractive. The signal itself (regardless of the +25 bps size) tells you the bank saw disorderly conditions. This is bearish for local equities in the short term (rates ↑ → discount rate ↑ → P/E multiples compress) but stabilizing medium-term.

Mechanism — Why acquirers "pay a premium" A control premium (typically 20–40% over unaffected share price) compensates target shareholders for giving up (a) future upside independent of the deal and (b) their diversification. The acquirer justifies it via synergies: cost synergies (removing duplicate HQ, IT, procurement scale) that are usually credible, and revenue synergies (cross-sell) that usually are not. In a pitch, always separate the two — juniors get in trouble by summing both at face value.

11 Learn — Concepts For Your Internship

Two concepts today. Small dose, high frequency.

Concept #1 — Discount rate & why rate cuts move equities
Why "Fed on hold" is a headwind for growth stocks
Every stock's price is (in theory) the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at some rate r.

Price = Σ CFt / (1+r)t

When r rises: near-term cash flows barely change, but far-in-the-future cash flows shrink dramatically (compounding works backwards). Growth stocks — companies whose cash flows are heavily back-loaded — get hit hardest. Value stocks (steady near-term CF) barely notice.

This is the entire reason yesterday looked the way it did: mega-cap tech (long duration) got sold; utilities, staples, industrials (short duration) rallied. Whenever you hear "rate expectations changed," translate it into "duration got repriced."
Concept #2 — EV / EBITDA in one page
Why bankers quote EV/EBITDA, not P/E
EV (Enterprise Value) = Market Cap + Debt − Cash. It's what you'd pay to buy the whole business, debt included, net of the cash you'd inherit.
EBITDA = earnings before interest, taxes, D&A. Approximation for operating cash flow, before capital structure choices.

Ratio EV/EBITDA answers: "for every dollar of operating cash the business throws off, how much am I paying for the entire capital stack?"

Why bankers prefer it to P/E:
  • Capital-structure-neutral — companies with different debt levels are still comparable (P/E gets distorted by interest expense).
  • Tax-neutral — cross-border comps work.
  • D&A-neutral — accounting choices on depreciation don't skew the comp.
Where it fails: capital-intensive businesses (telecom, airlines) — EBITDA overstates true cash flow because it ignores maintenance capex. For them, use EV/(EBITDA − Capex).
