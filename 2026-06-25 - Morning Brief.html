Learn Today · Internship Edge

one concept, fully explained — ~4 min

Why a 0.25% Fed hike can erase 10% of a stock's price — the DCF mechanism

If a manager asks you "why did tech sell off when the Fed paused?" — this is the answer they want to hear.

The core idea. A stock is worth the sum of all the cash it will ever produce, brought back to today's value using a "discount rate". The discount rate is essentially the risk-free rate (think 10-year Treasury yield, which moves with Fed policy) plus a risk premium. Higher discount rate → future cash flows are worth less today → stock price falls.

Intrinsic Value = Σ [ CashFlowt / (1 + r)t ]   where r = risk-free rate + equity risk premium

Why duration matters. A "long duration" stock is one whose cash flows are mostly in the far future — think a high-growth tech name making a loss today but projected to make billions in year 10. Because year-10 cash gets divided by (1+r)10, even a small bump in r compounds brutally. A "short duration" stock — a mature bank or a utility paying dividends now — barely feels the same rate move.

Worked example. Take a tech company expected to make $100 of free cash flow in year 10, growing 3% afterward.

  • If r = 8% → PV ≈ $100 / 1.0810 ÷ (8% − 3%) ≈ $926
  • If r = 8.25% (just 25 bps higher) → PV ≈ $859
  • That's a ~7% drop on a stock from a 25 bps move. For real growth names, the sensitivity is even higher.

Three practical takeaways for your internship:

  • When the 10-year UST yield moves 25 bps, expect a 5–10% move in unprofitable tech and a 1–2% move in defensives — that's the dispersion you'll see in your screen.
  • In a pitch, always state your WACC assumption and sensitivity. "We use 9% WACC; ±100 bps sensitivity moves price target by ±18%." This single sentence makes you sound senior.
  • If you're pitching long-duration growth in a rising-rate regime, you need a structural growth case strong enough to outweigh the discount-rate headwind — that's why Micron's +17% works (AI demand re-accelerates cash flows faster than rates dampen them).

Book of the Day

one chapter / day · build IB fluency in 90 days
Investment Banking — Rosenbaum & Pearl

Investment Banking: Valuation, LBOs, M&A, and IPOs

Joshua Rosenbaum & Joshua Pearl · 3rd edition (Wiley)

This is the technical bible used by analyst training programs at GS, MS, JPM, Citi. If you read one chapter a day and actually build the model alongside it, you will walk into your internship knowing more than 80% of incoming analysts.

How to read it (efficient route, given limited time):

  • Days 1–7: Ch.1 Comparable Companies Analysis — build the football field
  • Days 8–14: Ch.2 Precedent Transactions — learn premium/synergy math
  • Days 15–28: Ch.3 DCF — the core valuation muscle (tie it to today's "Learn" section)
  • Days 29–60: Ch.4–5 LBO & M&A — accretion/dilution, sources & uses, IRR sensitivity
  • Days 61–90: Re-build every model from scratch, no looking — that's how it sticks

Alternative when you need narrative, not formulas: Barbarians at the Gate (Burrough & Helyar) — the RJR Nabisco LBO. Read it on weekends to internalize how PE deals actually unfold in the boardroom.
