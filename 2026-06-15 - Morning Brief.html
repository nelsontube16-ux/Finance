The One Story That Moves Everything

Top of the Tape

US–Iran agree to framework deal to end the war — Strait of Hormuz reopens, oil collapses, equities print new ATHs.

President Trump announced a ceasefire framework with Iran covering an immediate cessation of hostilities (including Lebanon), lifting of the US naval blockade, the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, suspension of sanctions on Iranian oil sales, and release of ~$24bn in frozen Iranian assets. The Strait carries roughly 20% of global oil & gas — its closure since February has been the single largest macro risk variable globally.

The response was textbook risk-on. WTI crude fell below $81 (off ~22% from April's $106 peak). The S&P 500 jumped 1.65% to a new all-time high of 7,554; Nasdaq rallied 3.07%. Energy lagged; semis, mega-cap tech, and beaten-down EM led. Indonesia's IHSG surged 5.03%, banks and Barito Pacific–linked names absorbed the foreign flow.

So what for the pitch Every Q2 model with $100+ oil and a "Hormuz risk premium" embedded just got obsolete. Re-test your earnings models for airlines, EM consumer, Indonesian banks, and shipping (LNG carriers will de-rate). Treat this as a regime change, not a one-day pop — but watch implementation: the April ceasefire collapsed in 48 hours. Truth-test the deal by Wednesday before adding net exposure.

Daily Finance Lesson

From Today's News
For your internship toolkit

How an IPO actually prices — and why SpaceX's $75B book matters

The headline says SpaceX "IPO'd for $75B." But what does that mechanically mean? Here is the workflow every analyst, banker, and PE associate needs in their head — explained from zero.

1. Why a company IPOs. An Initial Public Offering converts privately-held equity into freely-tradable public stock. The company raises primary capital (new shares = cash to the firm) and/or existing shareholders sell secondary shares (cash to them, not the firm). SpaceX did both, mostly primary.

2. The bookrunning process. A syndicate of investment banks (here, 21 banks) is hired. The lead bookrunner manages a process called bookbuilding: institutional investors submit indications of interest (IOIs) — how many shares they'll buy and at what price — across a marketed price range (e.g. "$125–$140").

3. Roadshow. Management (CEO/CFO) meets institutional investors over ~10 days. Goal: convert IOIs into firm orders. The 1,500 retail event SpaceX held was an unusual nod to retail demand.

4. Pricing the deal. The night before the IPO, the syndicate looks at the order book. If demand at the top of the range is multiple-times oversubscribed (SpaceX was — common in mega-IPOs), they can price at or above the range. SpaceX priced at $135 — solidly in range.

5. Allocation. Banks do not give every investor every share they want. They favor long-term holders (mutual funds, sovereign wealth, pensions) and reward "indications of high conviction." This is why hedge funds and retail get crumbs of hot deals.

6. The pop. If the deal is priced below what the market clears at, the stock pops on Day 1. SpaceX opened at $150 (+11%) and ran to $175+ (+30%). This "leaving money on the table" debate is one of the oldest in IB — bankers argue a pop builds aftermarket support; founders argue it's an under-priced gift to allocated investors.

7. Greenshoe / Over-allotment option. The underwriters typically have a 15% over-allotment option ("Greenshoe") — they can sell 15% more shares at the IPO price if demand is strong. This is how a $65B deal becomes a $75B deal. SpaceX's $75B size implies the Greenshoe was fully exercised.

8. Lock-up period. Insiders typically can't sell for 90–180 days. The end of the lock-up is one of the most predictable supply shocks in markets — watch SpaceX around December 2026 / February 2027.

  • Try this: Pull the SpaceX S-1 (SEC filing). Find the "Use of Proceeds" section. Identify how much SpaceX itself raised vs. what insiders cashed out.
  • Then this: Calculate implied 2026E revenue multiple. At $1.75T valuation and rumored ~$25–30B revenue, that's ~60x revenue. Compare to peer multiples — that's the premium for narrative.
  • Finally: Look at the Day 1 close vs. the IPO price. The delta (~30%) is the "money left on the table" — quantify it in dollars. That's your interview answer for "tell me about a recent deal."

Howard Marks · ~20 chapters · 280 pages

If you have time for one book as a Finance student bound for IB / PE / hedge funds, this is the highest signal-to-noise on the list. Marks is the founder of Oaktree Capital ($170B+ AUM) and one of the few investors Warren Buffett openly reads. Each chapter is 10–15 pages — perfectly sized for a single morning sitting.

The "Illuminated" edition adds annotations from Joel Greenblatt, Christopher Davis, Paul Johnson, and Seth Klarman — you get five legendary investors arguing in the margins. The book teaches second-level thinking, risk vs. volatility, market cycles, and contrarianism — the four mental models that separate analysts who get promoted from those who get cut.

Today's chapter (suggested) Chapter 5 — "Understanding Risk." Read 12 pages with your coffee. Then map today's brief: Iran "deal" looks safe; what's the real risk? Answer in 3 bullets in a notebook. Do this for a year and your pattern recognition becomes elite.
