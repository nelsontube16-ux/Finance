05 · Indonesia

Indonesian Stock Market — IHSG

The biggest single catalyst this week sits 24 hours away.
⚠ Critical event — Tomorrow 23 Jun

MSCI Annual Market Classification Review verdict. Indonesia is on the watchlist for a downgrade from Emerging Market → Frontier Market. If that happens, mechanical passive outflows could hit ~US$13B (Goldman base case), with bear-case scenarios cited at up to US$60B.

How a reclassification mechanically works

  • Index inclusion = forced buying. When MSCI tags a country EM, every passive EM fund (ETFs, mutual funds tracking MSCI EM) is forced to hold weight in that country.
  • Reclassification to Frontier = forced selling. Frontier markets are a different, smaller benchmark with far less AUM tracking it. The delta in tracked assets is what gets sold.
  • Why Indonesia is at risk: MSCI flagged "weak visibility in shareholdings" and "signs of coordinated trading" — i.e. low free-float quality, governance & data flow concerns.
  • The pre-emptive defense: Bank Indonesia hiked +100 bps since May to defend the rupiah and slow capital flight — that's the central bank already bracing.

Losses concentrated in industrials, financials, property, basic materials — i.e. exactly the sectors a foreign-flow shock would hit hardest.

Knowledge Feed — One Concept Per Day

Today: Enterprise Value (EV) — the single most-misunderstood metric in IB interviews.
Concept · Valuation 101

Enterprise Value (EV): what you actually pay for the business

The problem with Market Cap. Market Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding. It only tells you what equity holders own. But when you buy a company, you also inherit its debt — and you get to use its cash. So Market Cap alone misprices what an acquirer truly pays.

EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash & Equivalents + Preferred Stock + Minority Interest

The intuition (in one sentence): EV is the price you'd pay if you bought 100% of the business and paid off all the debt the day after closing — net of the cash you'd find in the company's bank account on Day 1.

Why every multiple uses it: EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales, EV/EBIT — these compare apples to apples across companies with different capital structures. P/E does not: a heavily indebted company looks "cheap" on P/E because interest expense suppresses earnings, but it's still expensive on EV/EBITDA. That's why bankers default to EV multiples.

A worked example. Suppose Company X:

Market Cap = $500M · Debt = $200M · Cash = $50M EV = 500 + 200 − 50 = $650M If EBITDA = $100M → EV/EBITDA = 6.5×

An IB analyst who only quoted "Market Cap = $500M" would have lied about the real cost of the business. The acquirer pays $650M of enterprise value to take control.

The interview trap: "Why do we add minority interest?" Answer — because EBITDA in the denominator includes 100% of the subsidiary's earnings, but Market Cap only reflects the parent's share. To make numerator and denominator consistent, you add back the minority interest value.
