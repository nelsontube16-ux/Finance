08 · New Knowledge · For your internship

Enterprise Value vs. Equity Value — the concept you'll be tested on Day 1

Oppenheimer just downgraded Goldman "on valuation concerns." What does that even mean? To answer it you need to understand the two most misunderstood words in corporate finance: Enterprise Value and Equity Value. Nail this and you're ahead of 60% of your intern cohort.

The intuition — who owns what?

Imagine buying a house priced at $1,000,000 that comes with a $400,000 mortgage and $50,000 in cash in the kitchen drawer. The equity value — what you pay the owner — is $650,000 ($1m − $400k mortgage + $50k cash you're getting). But the true "cost of the whole house" — the enterprise value — is $1,000,000.

A company works the same way. To take it over you pay the shareholders (Equity Value) and you inherit the debt (you'll have to pay it off) minus the cash you'll pocket. That's Enterprise Value.

The formula

EV = Equity Value + Total Debt − Cash & Equivalents
      (+ Preferred Stock + Minority Interest, if any)

Equity Value = Share price × Shares outstanding (i.e., market cap, but always use diluted shares for M&A).

Why bankers use EV — matching numerator with denominator

Multiples are ratios. The number on top must speak to the same audience as the number on the bottom.

  • EV / EBITDA — EBITDA is earned before interest, so it belongs to everyone (debt + equity). Numerator must include debt too → EV.
  • EV / Revenue — Revenue is pre-everything → EV.
  • P / E — Net income is after interest, so it belongs only to shareholders → Equity Value (price) on top.
  • P / B — Book value of equity → Price.

Interview trap — "Why not P/EBITDA?" Because you'd be comparing an equity-holder claim (P) to a claim owned by debt + equity (EBITDA). Apples and oranges.

Applied to today's tape

When Oppenheimer flags "valuation" on GS, they're saying the P/E (Goldman is a bank, so P/E and P/TBV — Price to Tangible Book — are the industry-standard multiples, not EV/EBITDA, because banks' debt is their raw material). For an industrial or tech name in your pitch, you'd default to EV/EBITDA and EV/Revenue. Pick the multiple that fits the business model — that's the difference between a student and an analyst.

09 · Read Daily · 20 minutes

The one book to open every morning

Recommendation

Investment Banking: Valuation, LBOs, M&A, and IPOs — Rosenbaum & Pearl (3rd ed.)

This is the technical bible on every IB analyst's desk. Chapters map 1:1 to the work you'll do — comps, precedent transactions, DCF, LBO, merger consequences, and IPO mechanics. Each sub-section is ~20–30 minutes, self-contained. Read one per day, and by internship start you will understand a real pitch book.

How to read it in 20 min a day

  • Week 1–2 · Trading Comps — how to build a comp set and clean multiples.
  • Week 3 · Precedent Transactions — the M&A twin of trading comps.
  • Week 4–5 · DCF — cost of capital, terminal value, sensitivity tables.
  • Week 6–7 · LBO — the PE lens: sources & uses, IRR, credit metrics.
  • Week 8 · M&A / Merger Consequences — accretion / dilution.
  • Week 9 · IPOs — process, book-building, greenshoe.

Pair with — "Barbarians at the Gate" (Burrough & Helyar) as bedtime reading. Same material, story form. You'll never forget an LBO structure once you've watched KKR fight for RJR Nabisco.
