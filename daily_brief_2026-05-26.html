Indonesia · IDX / IHSG

03 · The crisis story
Indonesia · Currency & rates

BI's shock 50 bp hike — and why the rupiah kept falling anyway

First 50 bp move since 2022. Governor Perry Warjiyo cited "rupiah stabilization." The currency hit a record low of 17,600/USD the same week.

On May 20, Bank Indonesia surprised consensus (which expected 25 bp) by lifting the BI Rate to 5.25%. Rupiah is down ~5% YTD and at an all-time low against the dollar. BI simultaneously tightened rules on corporate dollar purchases — a soft form of capital control. The hike has yet to stabilize the currency, suggesting the market is repricing political risk, not just rate differentials.

Trigger: President Prabowo's move to establish a centralized export agency — perceived as raising costs and frictions for listed exporters. Fitch maintained a negative outlook on domestic banks, citing sovereign risk.

The setup

A textbook EM stress sequence: weak currency → forced policy hike → tighter financial conditions → equity drawdown → foreign selling → further currency weakness. The loop only breaks with credible fiscal anchoring or a global risk-on impulse.

Pitch angle: If you believe in mean reversion, the contrarian long basket is quality exporters with USD revenues (UNTR, INCO, MEDC) — they benefit from a weaker rupiah while the broader index suffers. The short basket is rate-sensitive consumer/property (BSDE, CTRA, BBCA on relative basis).

Today's Lesson

08 · For the intern in you
Practical · 6-minute read

Why an emerging market central bank can hike rates and still watch its currency fall

Indonesia is the live case study this week. BI surprise-hiked 50 bps to 5.25% on May 20 — and the rupiah hit a record low days later. Textbook says rate hikes attract capital and strengthen the currency. So why didn't it?

The intuition framework (memorize this — it shows up in every EM interview):

FX direction = f(rate differential) + f(political/credit risk premium) + f(global USD liquidity)

When rate hikes fail, it's because the second term (risk premium) dominates the first (carry). That's exactly what's happening here.

1

Carry channel (broken). A higher BI Rate widens the IDR–USD spread, which should attract carry traders. But carry only works when you trust the principal will be returned at the spot rate when you exit.

2

Risk premium channel (dominant). Prabowo's centralized export agency is read as state-encroachment on listed companies. Fitch's negative bank outlook compounds the perception that institutional rules are softening. Investors demand a higher premium for that risk than even 5.25% can pay.

3

Self-reinforcing loop. FX weakness → imported inflation → forced hikes → tighter financial conditions → equity drawdown → more foreign selling → more FX weakness. BI introducing dollar-purchase restrictions is a capital control by another name — which itself raises the risk premium further.

4

The break. The loop ends with one of three things: (a) credible fiscal anchoring from the government, (b) a global risk-on impulse (think Fed pivot or a benign Iran deal), or (c) full capital controls + IMF backstop. Watch which one comes first — that's your signal to flip the trade.

Why this matters for your pitch: when you write the Indonesia section of any equity research, the FX setup is the first page, not an appendix. Multi-nationals (Astra, Telkom) have IDR earnings but USD-linked input costs — they get hit twice. Pure USD-revenue miners (INCO, MEDC) actually benefit. The two groups should be on opposite sides of your trade book.

Daily Reading

09 · Build the muscle
Howard Marks
The Most Important Thing Illuminated
Columbia · 2013

The Most Important Thing Illuminated

Howard Marks · Co-Chairman, Oaktree Capital

Twenty short chapters, each one a principle. Built deliberately to be read in 10–15 minute sittings. Marks distills four decades of distressed-debt and PE judgment into chapters like "Second-Level Thinking," "The Most Important Thing Is Cycles," "Combating Negative Influences," and "The Role of Luck."

Unlike Investment Banking by Rosenbaum & Pearl (your technical bible — keep on the desk as a reference), Marks teaches you how to think, which is what differentiates a partner from an associate. The "Illuminated" edition adds commentary by Buffett, Klarman, and Bruce Berkowitz around each chapter, giving you three points of view per concept.

Why daily: Pair this with Marks' free Oaktree Memos (oaktreecapital.com/insights/memos) — published every 2–3 months but legendary. A chapter a day for 20 days, then loop. By the third loop, you'll start thinking in cycles automatically — which is the most valuable upgrade you can give your brain in this market regime.

Honorable mentions for later: Rosenbaum & Pearl (technical), Barbarians at the Gate by Burrough & Helyar (M&A war story), When Genius Failed by Lowenstein (risk management), The Outsiders by Thorndike (capital allocation).
