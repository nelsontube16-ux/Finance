Today's Finance Lesson

For your internship arsenal
Lesson 1 · The Single Most Important M&A Metric

EV/EBITDA — and why bankers live and die by it.

If you remember one valuation ratio walking into an IB interview, make it EV/EBITDA. Every deal pitch — Tate & Lyle, SFR, Flender — was priced off it. Here's the full mechanism, because you said no background:

1 · What is EV (Enterprise Value)?

EV is the price a buyer pays to acquire the entire business, not just its equity. Market cap only buys the shareholders out. To take a company private you also have to repay its debt and you "get back" its cash. So:

EV = Market Cap + Total Debt − Cash & Equivalents + Minority Interest + Preferred Stock

Why include debt? Because if you buy a company with $1B equity and $500M debt, you owe the lenders $500M after closing. The lenders sit ahead of you in the capital stack. Cash you subtract because the moment you own the business, that cash is yours and effectively reduces your purchase cost.

2 · What is EBITDA?

Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. Read it as cash operating profit — what the business generates before financing choices, tax jurisdiction, and accounting non-cash items distort the picture.

EBITDA = Net Income + Interest + Taxes + D&A or = Operating Income (EBIT) + D&A

Why bankers love it: it lets you compare a French chemicals firm vs. an Indonesian bank vs. a US tech firm without the noise of how each is financed or taxed.

3 · Why the ratio matters: it tells you how many "years of cash profit" you're paying for the business.

If a company's EV is $10B and EBITDA is $1B, then EV/EBITDA = 10x. You are paying 10 years of (pre-tax, pre-financing) operating cash flow for the asset. Lower = cheaper. Higher = priced for growth (or overvalued).

4 · Typical multiples to memorize

  • Mature industrials / consumer staples: 8–12x
  • Banks (note: bankers usually use P/BV instead): 6–10x
  • Software / SaaS: 15–30x (sometimes EV/Revenue is used)
  • Telcos / utilities: 6–9x
  • Biotech (early stage): N/A — DCF based on probability-weighted milestones

5 · Apply it to today's news

Ingredion / Tate & Lyle at $3.6B. Tate's TTM EBITDA ≈ $400M → implied EV/EBITDA ≈ 9x. That's a fair price for specialty ingredients — neither steal nor stretch. If Ingredion paid 12x, the market would have called it overpaying; if 6x, the board would be sued by Tate shareholders. The 9–10x zone is the bargaining battlefield for industrials M&A.

Action for you → Pick any one of today's deals. Pull the target's last 10-K, find EBITDA, compute the implied EV/EBITDA. Do this every day until it's reflex. By week 4 of your internship you'll be the only intern who can answer "where does this deal price?" in 30 seconds.

Investment Banking: Valuation, LBOs, M&A, and IPOs
⊳ Recommendation

Investment Banking — Joshua Rosenbaum & Joshua Pearl (3rd ed.)

This is the book every first-year analyst at Goldman, JPM, MS, and BCA Sekuritas has on their desk. It is structured exactly the way you'd be taught in training week: Comparable Companies → Precedent Transactions → DCF → LBO → M&A mechanics (accretion/dilution, deal structures) → IPO process. Each chapter is self-contained and ~30–50 pages — perfect for one chapter = one week of mornings, ~15 min/day.

Why this over Damodaran or "Barbarians at the Gate"? Damodaran is the academic encyclopedia (deeper, but longer). "Barbarians" is the war story (read it on a weekend for fun). Rosenbaum & Pearl is the only one that teaches you the exact mechanics you will be asked to execute on day 1 of an internship — building comps spreads, scrubbing EBITDA, running an LBO model. If you only read one book before your internship, this is it.

Daily plan: Chapter 1 (Comparable Companies Analysis) → 5 days × 15 min. Re-read Chapter 5 (M&A Sale Process) before any deal-flow meeting.
